Part A · A.1

Shoot the Pictures

The planned capture procedure keeps the camera's center of projection fixed and changes only its orientation. Adjacent views should overlap by approximately 40–70%, with clear, stationary features distributed throughout the overlap. Most photographs will be captured personally. A.1 requires at least two sets; I reserve three sets here because A.4 requires three mosaics.

Why Camera Rotation Matters

With a fixed camera center, a scene point is observed along the same spatial ray before and after rotation. Its depth cancels, so a single homography can relate the two images. If the intrinsic calibration is unchanged and R maps camera-1 coordinates into camera-2 coordinates, the ideal relationship is:

\[ \mathbf p_2 \sim H\mathbf p_1, \qquad H=KRK^{-1}. \]

Here, K is the intrinsic camera matrix and \(\sim\) means equality up to a nonzero scale. Moving the camera sideways introduces depth-dependent parallax, especially for nearby objects, which one homography generally cannot remove. Static, distant scenes and minimal camera translation make the model more appropriate.

I will avoid strong lens distortion and capture each set close together in time. Keeping exposure and focal length consistent also helps reduce differences that geometric alignment alone cannot correct.

Part A · A.2

Recover Homographies

I manually select the same features in the source and reference images, preserving their order. The default is 12 pairs, rather than the minimum four, so the system is overdetermined. Points should cover the overlap instead of clustering in one small area or lying on one line.

Projective Mapping

Points use (x, y) coordinates, while image arrays are indexed as [y, x]. A source-to-reference homography has eight independent parameters after fixing its bottom-right entry to one:

\[ \lambda\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix} h_{11}&h_{12}&h_{13}\\ h_{21}&h_{22}&h_{23}\\ h_{31}&h_{32}&1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{bmatrix}. \] \[ \begin{aligned} u&=\frac{h_{11}x+h_{12}y+h_{13}}{h_{31}x+h_{32}y+1},\\ v&=\frac{h_{21}x+h_{22}y+h_{23}}{h_{31}x+h_{32}y+1}. \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying out the denominators gives two linear constraints per correspondence. Stacking N pairs produces a \(2N\times8\) system:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_i&y_i&1&0&0&0&-u_i x_i&-u_i y_i\\ 0&0&0&x_i&y_i&1&-v_i x_i&-v_i y_i \end{bmatrix}}_{A_i} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} h_{11}\\h_{12}\\h_{13}\\h_{21}\\h_{22}\\h_{23}\\h_{31}\\h_{32} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf h} =\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}u_i\\v_i\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf b_i}. \]

Normalization and Least Squares

Large pixel coordinates can make the system poorly conditioned. Before constructing the system used by the solver, I normalize each point set independently: translate its centroid to the origin and scale its mean distance from the origin to \(\sqrt{2}\).

\[ \mathbf c=\frac1N\sum_i\mathbf p_i, \quad d=\frac1N\sum_i\lVert\mathbf p_i-\mathbf c\rVert_2, \quad s=\frac{\sqrt2}{d}, \quad T=\begin{bmatrix}s&0&-sc_x\\0&s&-sc_y\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}. \] \[ \widetilde{\mathbf h}^{\,*} =\underset{\widetilde{\mathbf h}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}} \lVert\widetilde A\widetilde{\mathbf h}-\widetilde{\mathbf b}\rVert_2^2, \qquad H=T_{\mathrm{ref}}^{-1}\widetilde H T_{\mathrm{src}}. \]

The implementation uses numpy.linalg.lstsq on the normalized system, restores the original coordinate systems, and rescales H so that \(h_{33}=1\). This minimizes an algebraic least-squares objective; it is not a nonlinear minimization of geometric reprojection error.

Part A · A.3

Warp the Images and Rectify Planes

A Common Canvas and Inverse Mapping

I treat integer coordinates as pixel centers. An image of width W and height H therefore has corner centers at (0, 0), (W − 1, 0), (W − 1, H − 1), and (0, H − 1). Projecting the corners gives the bounds of a finite planar warp. I round the minimum coordinates down and the maximum coordinates up, including both endpoint pixels.

\[ W_{\mathrm{out}}=x_{\max}-x_{\min}+1, \qquad H_{\mathrm{out}}=y_{\max}-y_{\min}+1. \] \[ \mathbf q_{\mathrm{ref}}= \begin{bmatrix}j+x_{\min}\\i+y_{\min}\\1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix}X_s\\Y_s\\Z_s\end{bmatrix} =H^{-1}\mathbf q_{\mathrm{ref}}, \qquad (x_s,y_s)=\left(\frac{X_s}{Z_s},\frac{Y_s}{Z_s}\right). \]

For each output pixel [i, j], I find its continuous source coordinate and sample there. This inverse procedure avoids the holes produced by scattering source pixels onto a destination grid. The canvas origin may be negative; its offset must be added before applying the inverse matrix.

A separate geometric mask records whether the source coordinate is finite and lies inside the image. The mask does not depend on color, so a genuinely black source pixel remains valid. Uncovered output pixels are saved with zero alpha.

Two Interpolation Methods from Scratch

Nearest-neighbor interpolation rounds the source coordinate to the closest pixel center:

\[ I_{\mathrm{NN}}(x,y)= I\!\left(\left\lfloor x+\tfrac12\right\rfloor, \left\lfloor y+\tfrac12\right\rfloor\right). \]

Bilinear interpolation uses the four neighboring pixels. For \(x_0=\lfloor x\rfloor\), \(y_0=\lfloor y\rfloor\), \(a=x-x_0\), and \(b=y-y_0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} I_{\mathrm{bil}}(x,y)={}&(1-a)(1-b)I(x_0,y_0) +a(1-b)I(x_0+1,y_0)\\ &+(1-a)bI(x_0,y_0+1)+abI(x_0+1,y_0+1). \end{aligned} \]

The implementation clamps the upper neighbor at the final row or column and applies the same weights independently to each color channel. Nearest neighbor preserves discrete samples but can produce jagged edges. Bilinear usually gives smoother transitions, at the cost of more arithmetic and some softening. Actual timings and visual differences remain to be measured; bilinear interpolation alone is not a complete antialiasing solution for strong downscaling.

Rectification

A known rectangle supplies a simple geometric test. I select its corners in perimeter order—top left, top right, bottom right, bottom left—and map them to a front-facing rectangle. The output width-to-height ratio should match the object's known proportions. A square target is appropriate for a square tile, but not for every rectangular object.

\[ \mathbf p_1,\mathbf p_2,\mathbf p_3,\mathbf p_4 \longrightarrow (0,0),\ (W_r-1,0),\ (W_r-1,H_r-1),\ (0,H_r-1). \]

Here four corner pairs define the desired plane-to-plane mapping. I render both methods with the same H and output dimensions, time only the warp computation, and save the two images separately for comparison.

Part A · A.4

Blend the Images into Mosaics

I choose a reference image and express every other view in that coordinate system. A directly matched pair supplies its homography; for a sequence of matches, the matrices are composed along a path to the reference:

\[ H_{i\rightarrow r}=H_{j\rightarrow r}H_{i\rightarrow j}. \]

The implementation stores both directions of each pair and traverses the connected group from the reference image. The reference itself uses the identity transform. All transformed image corners determine one shared canvas, and every image is inverse-warped into it using bilinear sampling.

Feathering Rather Than Overwriting

Overwriting one view with another creates an abrupt boundary. I assign a larger weight to pixels farther from an image's edges, then normalize the combined contributions. For source image i with dimensions \(W_i\times H_i\), the implemented weight is:

\[ d_i(x,y)=\min\{x+1,\ W_i-x,\ y+1,\ H_i-y\}, \qquad w_i(x,y)=\frac{d_i(x,y)}{\max_{x,y}d_i(x,y)}. \]

The +1 convention keeps weights positive on valid boundary pixels. RGB and the weight map are packed together and warped with the same coordinates, so the weights remain aligned with their colors. Let \(\bar I_i\) and \(\bar w_i\) be the warped image and weight, and \(M_i\) its validity mask:

\[ I_{\mathrm{mosaic}}(p)= \frac{\sum_i M_i(p)\bar w_i(p)\bar I_i(p)} {\sum_i M_i(p)\bar w_i(p)}, \qquad \text{where the denominator is positive.} \]

A pixel covered by only one view keeps that view's color. Overlapping views contribute a weighted average, while uncovered pixels remain transparent. Feathering reduces abrupt seams but cannot repair incorrect geometry, parallax, or moving objects; averaging misaligned details can still produce ghosting.

Part A · A.5 · Optional extension for CS180

Cylindrical Projection

A planar mosaic stretches views toward the sides. The optional implementation instead represents the same aligned views on a cylinder. Relative to the reference image center \((c_x,c_y)\), write \(X=x-c_x\), \(Y=y-c_y\), and choose a focal length f in processed-image pixels:

\[ u=c_x+f\arctan(X/f), \qquad v=c_y+\frac{fY}{\sqrt{f^2+X^2}}. \]

To render the output, I invert this mapping before applying each source image's inverse homography:

\[ \theta=\frac{u-c_x}{f}, \qquad x=c_x+f\tan\theta, \qquad y=c_y+\frac{v-c_y}{\cos\theta}, \qquad |\theta|<\frac\pi2. \]

Each cylindrical output pixel is mapped back through the reference plane to a source view and sampled directly. This avoids rendering a planar mosaic and then geometrically resampling that rendered image again. I also check extrema along the transformed image edges because curved boundaries can extend beyond the mapped corners.

This extension uses the existing planar alignment and a chosen focal length; it does not implement a calibrated, full 360-degree panorama. The focal length and the comparison below will be selected after the real photographs are available.

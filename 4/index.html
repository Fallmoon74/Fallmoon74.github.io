Image Warping and Mosaicing

Manual and automatic alignment of overlapping photographs

Part A

Image Warping and Mosaicing

Part A · A.1

Shoot the Pictures

I took two overlapping photos for each scene. I resized them to a maximum of 1600 pixels on the long side and used the second photo as the reference.

Rotating the Camera

I tried to rotate the camera without moving it sideways. This lets one homography align the two views. With camera matrix K and rotation R:

\[ \mathbf p_2 \sim H\mathbf p_1, \qquad H=KRK^{-1}. \]

K describes the camera, and \(\sim\) means the points are equal up to scale. Moving the camera sideways can make nearby and distant objects shift differently, so they may not all line up.

Part A · A.2

Recover Homographies

I picked the same features in both photos and used local normalized cross-correlation to refine the points. I used 12 pairs for the residential street, 10 for the California sushi, and 22 for the street corner.

Projective Mapping

I store points as (x, y). The homography H maps a point to the other image. Setting its last entry to 1 leaves eight values to solve for:

\[ \lambda\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix} h_{11}&h_{12}&h_{13}\\ h_{21}&h_{22}&h_{23}\\ h_{31}&h_{32}&1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{bmatrix}. \] \[ \begin{aligned} u&=\frac{h_{11}x+h_{12}y+h_{13}}{h_{31}x+h_{32}y+1},\\ v&=\frac{h_{21}x+h_{22}y+h_{23}}{h_{31}x+h_{32}y+1}. \end{aligned} \]

Each matching pair gives two equations. With N pairs, I solve a \(2N\times8\) system:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_i&y_i&1&0&0&0&-u_i x_i&-u_i y_i\\ 0&0&0&x_i&y_i&1&-v_i x_i&-v_i y_i \end{bmatrix}}_{A_i} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} h_{11}\\h_{12}\\h_{13}\\h_{21}\\h_{22}\\h_{23}\\h_{31}\\h_{32} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf h} =\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}u_i\\v_i\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf b_i}. \]

Normalizing the Points

To make the solve more stable, I center each point set and scale its average distance to \(\sqrt{2}\). I fit H with least squares, then convert it back to the original coordinates:

\[ \mathbf c=\frac1N\sum_i\mathbf p_i, \quad d=\frac1N\sum_i\lVert\mathbf p_i-\mathbf c\rVert_2, \quad s=\frac{\sqrt2}{d}, \quad T=\begin{bmatrix}s&0&-sc_x\\0&s&-sc_y\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}. \] \[ \widetilde{\mathbf h}^{\,*} =\underset{\widetilde{\mathbf h}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}} \lVert\widetilde A\widetilde{\mathbf h}-\widetilde{\mathbf b}\rVert_2^2, \qquad H=T_{\mathrm{ref}}^{-1}\widetilde H T_{\mathrm{src}}. \]

Part A · A.3

Warp the Images and Rectify Planes

Canvas and Inverse Warping

I transform the image corners to find a canvas that fits the result. I round the bounds outward and include both end pixels:

\[ W_{\mathrm{out}}=x_{\max}-x_{\min}+1, \qquad H_{\mathrm{out}}=y_{\max}-y_{\min}+1. \] \[ \mathbf q_{\mathrm{ref}}= \begin{bmatrix}j+x_{\min}\\i+y_{\min}\\1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix}X_s\\Y_s\\Z_s\end{bmatrix} =H^{-1}\mathbf q_{\mathrm{ref}}, \qquad (x_s,y_s)=\left(\frac{X_s}{Z_s},\frac{Y_s}{Z_s}\right). \]

For each output pixel, I use the inverse homography to find where to sample in the source image. This avoids leaving holes. The canvas offset is added before applying H inverse.

Nearest Neighbor and Bilinear

Nearest neighbor takes the closest source pixel:

\[ I_{\mathrm{NN}}(x,y)= I\!\left(\left\lfloor x+\tfrac12\right\rfloor, \left\lfloor y+\tfrac12\right\rfloor\right). \]

Bilinear interpolation averages four neighboring pixels. Let \(x_0=\lfloor x\rfloor\), \(y_0=\lfloor y\rfloor\), \(a=x-x_0\), and \(b=y-y_0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} I_{\mathrm{bil}}(x,y)={}&(1-a)(1-b)I(x_0,y_0) +a(1-b)I(x_0+1,y_0)\\ &+(1-a)bI(x_0,y_0+1)+abI(x_0+1,y_0+1). \end{aligned} \]

I wrote both methods from scratch. Nearest neighbor is faster but can look jagged. Bilinear takes more work and usually looks smoother.

Rectification

I picked four corners in order: top left, top right, bottom right, and bottom left. I mapped them to a front-facing rectangle with the same shape as the object:

\[ \mathbf p_1,\mathbf p_2,\mathbf p_3,\mathbf p_4 \longrightarrow (0,0),\ (W_r-1,0),\ (W_r-1,H_r-1),\ (0,H_r-1). \]

I used the same H and output size for both methods. The timings below measure only the image warp.

Part A · A.4

Blend the Images into Mosaics

I keep one photo fixed and warp the other onto a larger canvas.

Blending the Images

I blend the overlapping colors instead of replacing one photo with the other. Pixels near the center get more weight. In the formula, \(I_i\) is a warped photo and \(w_i\) is its weight, set to zero outside the photo:

\[ I_{\mathrm{mosaic}}(p)= \frac{\sum_i w_i(p)I_i(p)} {\sum_i w_i(p)}. \]

This makes the seam less sharp. Misaligned details can still look doubled, and empty areas stay transparent.

Part A · A.5 · Optional extension for CS180

Cylindrical Projection

I also tried a cylinder to reduce stretching at the sides. Here, \((c_x,c_y)\) is the image center, \(X=x-c_x\), \(Y=y-c_y\), and f is the focal length in pixels:

\[ u=c_x+f\arctan(X/f), \qquad v=c_y+\frac{fY}{\sqrt{f^2+X^2}}. \]

I sample the source photos directly and blend them in the same way. I used f = 1200 pixels for this comparison, with the same photos and H as the planar result.

The cylinder makes the left house and cars look less stretched. The top and bottom edges become curved. Some doubled details remain.

Part B

Automatic Correspondence Finding

Part B · B.1

Harris Corners and ANMS

I used the Harris code provided by the teacher, with a Gaussian scale of 1 pixel. It finds corners where the image changes in both directions:

\[ M=G_1*\begin{bmatrix}I_x^2&I_xI_y\\I_xI_y&I_y^2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad R=\frac{2\det M}{\operatorname{tr}M+\epsilon}. \]

I remove points near the image boundary and keep peaks with \(R_i>0.005R_{\max}\). The strongest points often cluster together, so I use ANMS to spread them out:

\[ r_i=\min_{j:\,R_i<0.9R_j}\lVert\mathbf p_i-\mathbf p_j\rVert_2. \]

The radius is the distance to a stronger corner. If there is none, I set it to infinity. I keep the 500 largest radii. This is my chosen count, not a fixed assignment requirement.

Part B · B.2

Feature Descriptors

I blur the grayscale image with a Gaussian scale of 2 pixels. Around each point, I sample an 8 × 8 patch from a 40 × 40 area, taking one sample every 5 pixels:

\[ I_b=G_2*I_{\mathrm{gray}}, \qquad u,v\in\{0,\ldots,7\}, \] \[ P_i[v,u]=I_b(x_i+5u-17.5,\ y_i+5v-17.5). \]

I subtract the patch mean and divide by its standard deviation to reduce brightness and contrast differences. I then flatten it into 64 numbers:

\[ \mathbf d_i=\operatorname{vec}\!\left(\frac{P_i-\mu_i}{s_i}\right) \in\mathbb R^{64}. \]

I keep the patches aligned with the image axes. All 500 selected points in each photo gave valid descriptors.

Part B · B.3

Match the Features

I compare patches using SSD, the sum of squared differences. A smaller value means the patches look more alike. I keep the best match only if its SSD is less than 0.6 times the second-best SSD:

\[ D_{ij}=\lVert\mathbf d_i-\mathbf d'_j\rVert_2^2, \qquad D_{i,j_1}\leq D_{i,j_2}\leq\cdots, \] \[ \frac{D_{i,j_1}}{D_{i,j_2}}<0.6. \]

The ratio uses squared distances. It removes many unclear matches, but some wrong ones remain. These points are found automatically.

Part B · B.4

RANSAC and Automatic Mosaics

I wrote four-point RANSAC from scratch. Each trial picks four matches and computes H with the Part A solver. I run 10,000 trials and skip samples with three points on one line.

I check how far each point misses its match in both directions and use the larger error. A tilde adds a 1 to a point, and \(\pi\) divides by the last coordinate:

\[ \begin{aligned} e_i^{\to}&=\lVert\pi(H\widetilde{\mathbf p}_i)-\mathbf q_i\rVert_2,\\ e_i^{\leftarrow}&=\lVert\pi(H^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf q}_i)-\mathbf p_i\rVert_2,\\ e_i(H)&=\max(e_i^{\to},e_i^{\leftarrow}). \end{aligned} \] \[ \mathcal I(H)=\{i:e_i(H)<3\}, \qquad H^*=\underset{H}{\operatorname{arg\,max}}\,|\mathcal I(H)|. \]

I keep matches with error below 3 pixels. I choose the H with the most inliers and fit it again using those points, up to five times. Then I warp and blend the photos with the Part A code.

A Difficult Pair: California Sushi

The sushi photos share only a small area, and the fur patches look very similar. RANSAC did not find a useful alignment. Raising the ratio threshold to 0.8 or 0.9 did not help, so I used the other three scenes for the automatic mosaics.

RANSAC removes many wrong matches, but it still needs enough good ones. It also cannot fix objects moving or the camera shifting sideways.

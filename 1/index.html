Part 1

Single-scale Alignment with NCC

For the three smaller JPG images, a single-scale NCC algorithm is sufficient. Each original scan contains three vertically stacked grayscale exposures. These exposures record the scene brightness through blue, green, and red filters, respectively. Simply placing them into the three channels of an RGB image produces colored outlines because the three exposures are not aligned.

The code structure first split the scan into equal B, G, and R sections and use the blue channel as the reference. Then exhaustively shift the green and red channels over a small window of candidate (dy, dx) displacements. At every candidate position, NCC measures the similarity between the reference and shifted channel.

NCC subtracts the mean intensity from each image region and treats the remaining pixel values as vectors. It then divides their dot product by the product of their lengths. This normalization makes the score less sensitive to differences in average brightness. A score closer to 1 indicates that the two channel patterns are more similar.

\[ \operatorname{NCC}(I,J)= \frac{\sum_p (I_p-\bar I)(J_p-\bar J)} {\sqrt{\sum_p (I_p-\bar I)^2}\sqrt{\sum_p (J_p-\bar J)^2}} \]

Here, I is the blue reference region, J is a candidate shifted region, and p indexes the pixels. I select the displacement that produces the highest NCC score. Border pixels are excluded from scoring so that the glass plate edges and pixels wrapped around by np.roll do not dominate the comparison.

Before Border Handling

My first implementation applied NCC directly to almost the entire image. The high-contrast plate borders contributed strongly to the similarity score, so the algorithm sometimes aligned the borders instead of the actual scene. This produced visible red, green, and blue blurring around the church and the tree line. After excluding the border region from the NCC calculation, the three aligned much more closely.

Part 2

Multi-scale Image Pyramid

The TIFF scans are much larger than the JPG images, and their correct displacements can exceed one hundred pixels. Exhaustively testing every large displacement at full resolution would require many expensive NCC evaluations. To make this practical, I use a coarse-to-fine image pyramid.

At the smallest level, I search a relatively wide window of ±30 pixels and select the displacement with the highest NCC score. A displacement found on this small image represents a much larger displacement in the original image.

Fine-level Refinement

When moving to the next finer level, I first double the previous displacement. I then search only for a local correction around that prediction:

\[ \widetilde{\mathbf d}^{(l)}=2\mathbf d^{(l+1)}, \qquad \mathbf d^{(l)}= \widetilde{\mathbf d}^{(l)}+ \underset{\boldsymbol\delta\in[-r_l,r_l]^2}{\operatorname{arg\,max}} \operatorname{NCC}\!\left( B^{(l)}, T_{\widetilde{\mathbf d}^{(l)}+\boldsymbol\delta}\!\left(C^{(l)}\right) \right) \]

With four downsampling steps, the correction radius becomes smaller toward full resolution: approximately 15, 8, 4, and finally 2 pixels. This preserves the large motion discovered at the coarse level while allowing each finer level to correct rounding and downsampling errors. The process is performed independently for green-to-blue and red-to-blue alignment, and the level-0 displacements are applied to the original channels.

Before building the pyramid, I remove 10% from each side of a copy of every channel used for scoring. The final offsets are then applied to the full-resolution, uncropped channels. This prevents the glass plate borders from controlling the NCC score without discarding image content from the final colorized result.

Bells & Whistles

Automatic Cropping

I detect borders independently in the B, G, and R plates, then use the largest crop on each side for all three channels. The detector combines two signals:

  • Near-black bands: consecutive rows or columns where at least 85% of pixels are below a dark-intensity threshold.
  • Intensity jumps: the first unusually strong change between adjacent rows or columns near an outer edge.

For a row i, the near-black ratio is the fraction of its pixels whose intensity is at most 0.05. The same calculation is applied to columns:

\[ q_i=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{x=1}^{W}\mathbf{1}\!\left[I(i,x)\leq 0.05\right], \qquad \text{black border if }q_i\geq 0.85 \]

For jump detection, I first smooth the channel and compute the mean absolute difference between adjacent rows. A jump is accepted only when it exceeds both a fixed minimum and an adaptive threshold based on the median absolute deviation:

\[ j_i=\frac{1}{W_c}\sum_{x\in\text{center }80\%} \left|\widetilde I(i+1,x)-\widetilde I(i,x)\right| \] \[ \tau=\max\!\left(0.03,\;\operatorname{median}(j) +6\operatorname{median}\!\left(|j-\operatorname{median}(j)|\right)\right) \]

For each side, I keep the larger boundary found by the black-band and jump detectors. I then take the largest boundary among the B, G, and R channels so that the same safe crop is applied to all three。

I still use a fixed 10% interior crop for alignment. Automatic border detection is applied to the final output, and the invalid regions introduced by channel shifts are removed as well.

Automatic Contrast

After cropping, I compute the 1st and 99th percentile intensities over the RGB image. The main intensity range is mapped to [0, 1], while values outside it are clipped. Ignoring the darkest and brightest 1% reduces the influence of isolated black scratches or white exposure artifacts.

\[ l=P_1(I),\qquad h=P_{99}(I),\qquad I'=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{I-l}{h-l},0,1\right) \]

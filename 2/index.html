Part 1.1

Convolutions from Scratch

I implemented convolution with four nested loops, then with two loops and NumPy patch multiplication. Both flip the kernel and use zero padding, matching SciPy's same/fill output.

# Four-loop core (after flipping and padding the kernel/image)
for row in range(height):
    for col in range(width):
        for kr in range(kernel_height):
            for kc in range(kernel_width):
                output[row, col] += padded[row + kr, col + kc] * kernel[kr, kc]

# Two-loop core
for row in range(height):
    for col in range(width):
        patch = padded[row:row + kernel_height, col:col + kernel_width]
        output[row, col] = np.sum(patch * kernel)

Both matched SciPy on a 200 × 200 crop. One run took 0.85 s, 0.072 s, and 0.0036 s (four loops, two loops, SciPy) respectively. Zero padding darkens the filtered borders, while reflection padding would not.

The blur looks mild because the 9 × 9 kernel is tiny relative to the 4284 × 5712 photo. It removes fine pixel-scale detail, but that change is hard to see once the image is reduced for the webpage.

Part 1.2

Finite-Difference Edges

I computed horizontal and vertical derivatives, then their gradient magnitude, sqrt(Dx² + Dy²). Threshold 0.15 suppresses noise while retaining strong contours, at the cost of faint edges.

Parameter tuning: I compared binary edges at different thresholds. Lower values kept more weak edges but also more speckles, and raising the threshold cleaned the background. I kept 0.15 as the balance for this image.

Part 1.3

Derivative of Gaussian

A 9 × 9 Gaussian (σ = 1.5) comes from the outer product of a 1D kernel with itself. Blurring first reduces noisy edges, and threshold 0.06 retains the stronger smoothed contours.

Parameter tuning: Smoothing reduces gradient magnitudes, so the 0.15 threshold from Part 1.2 would discard too many edges here. I used 0.06 after the 9 × 9, σ = 1.5 blur to keep the main contours while reducing noise.

Compared with Part 1.2, the speckles in the grass largely disappear and the main silhouette is cleaner, but some faint texture edges are lost.

Convolving the Gaussian with Dx and Dy creates two DoG kernels. In the image interior, one DoG convolution matches blur-then-differentiate.

Part 2.1

Unsharp Masking

High frequencies equal original − Gaussian blur. The single unsharp filter (1 + α)δ − αG adds them back. Larger α strengthens edges but can introduce halos.

Taj Mahal and Sharpening Strength

Parameter tuning: I compared α = 0.5, 1, and 2 with the same 9 × 9 Gaussian (σ = 1.5). Stronger α makes architectural edges clearer but also amplifies halos and noise, and α = 1 is the middle setting used for the dog.

Part 2.2

Hybrid Images

Each hybrid combines aligned high- and low-frequency images. Fine edges dominate nearby, and the low-frequency subject becomes clearer from farther away.

Derek + Nutmeg

Nutmeg: high-pass σ = 4, gain 1.5. Derek: low-pass σ = 8, weight 0.5.

Parameter tuning: I adjusted the two cutoffs to make Nutmeg's details visible up close and Derek's face readable from afar. The 1.5 high-pass gain strengthened the faint cat edges, and the 0.5 low-pass weight kept them from being washed out.

Dog + Brad Pitt

Dog: high-pass σ = 5. Portrait: low-pass σ = 2. Their mismatched shapes make alignment difficult.

Parameter tuning: I used a stronger high-pass cutoff for the dog's texture and a smaller low-pass σ so the portrait did not become too diffuse. The remaining overlap issue comes from the different subject shapes, not just the filter settings.

Eva + Shinji: Frequency Analysis

Eva: high-pass σ = 4, gain 1.5. Shinji: low-pass σ = 2. This keeps Eva's lines and Shinji's broad face shape.

Parameter tuning: I increased Eva's high-frequency contribution until her linework was visible without overpowering Shinji. A modest low-pass σ kept Shinji recognizable at reduced size.

These log-magnitude FFTs show the aligned inputs, filters, and hybrid. High-pass filtering removes central energy, and low-pass filtering concentrates it.

Part 2.3

Gaussian and Laplacian Stacks

Gaussian levels blur progressively, so don't downsampling. Laplacian levels subtract adjacent Gaussian images, and their sum, including the coarsest residual, get into the final blended image.

A blurred half-image mask mixes the apple and orange at each scale. The 4 × 3 grid recreates Figure 3.42, ending with the oraple.

Part 2.4

Multiresolution Blending

Each image contributes a Laplacian stack, and the mask contributes a Gaussian stack. Weighted bands combine at each level, then sum into the final image. As the result, Sharp high-frequency masks preserve detail and broad low-frequency masks soften color and lighting seams.

Vertical Seam: Shinji + Eva

The direct center cut is obvious, and multiresolution blending softens it a little.

Parameter tuning: Six levels with mask blur starting at σ = 2 keep the fine seam relatively sharp while widening the transition for color and lighting at coarser levels.

Irregular Mask 1: Cosmic Eye

The mask follows the iris but excludes the pupil. Blending softens the direct composite's sticker-like border.

Parameter tuning: I tried moon first, but the image has unwanted cloud edges, and only moon is far more different from the night sky, thus the result was unsatisfactory. I switched to an iris-shaped mask. For the smaller eye region, five levels and a narrower starting mask blur (σ = 1.5) soften the boundary without spreading the nebula across the whole eye.

Irregular Mask 2: Universe Between the Hands

The mask follows the heart-shaped opening. Blending softens its sky-to-nebula edge without changing the hands.

Parameter tuning: I kept the opening inside the hands and used the six-level, σ = 2 mask schedule. This blends the sky and nebula at broad scales while leaving the hand contours intact.
